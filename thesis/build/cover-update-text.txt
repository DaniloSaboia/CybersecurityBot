                                       Resumo

Durante a resposta a um incidente de cibersegurança, encontrar informação não basta:
é necessário reconhecer a orientação aplicável, compreender as suas condições e decidir
como a utilizar. Quando procedimentos, relatórios de incidentes e informação sobre ame-
aças se encontram dispersos, a experiência acumulada pode ser difícil de recuperar no
momento em que é necessária. Neste trabalho, investigamos como um sistema de ges-
tão de conhecimento pode utilizar machine learning para apoiar essa consulta e tornar o
conhecimento disponível mais útil para a decisão.
    Adotamos uma abordagem de investigação orientada à conceção, ligando a análise do
problema à definição de requisitos, ao desenvolvimento de uma solução e à sua avalia-
ção. Consideramos o ciclo de gestão do conhecimento, desde a recolha e organização até
à atualização e reutilização. O papel da aprendizagem automática é estudado na recu-
peração de informação relevante e na formulação de orientações baseadas em fontes. A
responsabilidade pela decisão mantém-se com o analista.
    Definimos a otimização da resposta a incidentes através da qualidade da informação
recuperada, da adequação das orientações e do esforço necessário para realizar as tarefas.
A avaliação relaciona estas dimensões com a experiência de utilização, sem reduzir o
desempenho a uma única medida de rapidez. Procuramos, assim, aproximar a gestão de
conhecimento das necessidades da resposta a incidentes, preservando a possibilidade de
consultar as fontes e respeitando a confidencialidade da informação.
Palavras-chave: gestão de conhecimento; aprendizagem automática; resposta a inciden-
tes; cibersegurança; recuperação de informação.

                                       Abstract

During a cybersecurity incident, finding information is only part of the task. An an-
alyst must identify applicable guidance, understand its conditions and decide how to
use it. When procedures, incident reports and threat information are dispersed, accu-
mulated experience may be diﬀicult to recover when it is needed. We investigate how
a knowledge-management system can use machine learning to support this process and
make documented knowledge more useful for incident-response decisions.
    We adopt a design-science approach that connects the problem analysis with require-
ments, development and evaluation. Our scope covers the knowledge-management cycle,
including collection, organisation, maintenance and reuse. We examine machine learn-
ing as a means of retrieving relevant information and formulating source-based guidance,
while leaving responsibility for operational decisions with the analyst.
    We define optimisation through the quality of retrieved information, the suitability of
the guidance and the effort required to complete a task. The evaluation connects these
dimensions with the experience of using the system, rather than reducing performance
to a single speed measure. This perspective places knowledge management within the
practical demands of incident response: making relevant experience accessible, retaining
the means to examine its sources and respecting information confidentiality.
Keywords: knowledge management; machine learning; incident response; cybersecurity;
information retrieval.

                                    Introduction

Responding to a cybersecurity incident requires decisions about what has happened, which
assets are affected and what action is appropriate. Analysts need to interpret the avail-
able evidence, relate it to operational conditions and select a defensible response. This
dissertation investigates how knowledge management supported by machine learning can
assist that work by making relevant knowledge easier to find, assess and reuse.

1.1. Motivation and context

Incident response involves detecting and analysing incidents, limiting their impact and
restoring affected operations. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)
places these activities within wider cybersecurity risk management, connecting response
with preparation and continuous improvement. Its recommendations emphasise the use
of incident experience to improve procedures and inform subsequent decisions [13]. This
relationship between action and learning provides the starting point for the present re-
search: knowledge produced through one investigation should remain useful when another
incident requires a response.
    Consider an analyst investigating a suspected account compromise. An external advi-
sory may describe the threat, an internal procedure may specify the permitted response,
and a previous incident report may explain why a similar action was ineffective in a
particular environment. These sources answer different parts of the same question. To
use them together, the analyst needs to establish their relevance, recognise their limita-
tions and understand whether the circumstances of the earlier case still apply. Finding a
document is only part of this task; interpreting it is also necessary.
    We use knowledge management to mean the deliberate capture, organisation, mainte-
nance and reuse of knowledge for this decision-making purpose. The knowledge of interest
includes documented procedures, explanations of earlier decisions and lessons derived from
incident experience. Capturing an outcome without its rationale can leave later readers
unable to judge its applicability. Maintaining that context gives knowledge reuse a more
specific purpose than accumulating documents.
    Knowledge-sharing research considers both the factors affecting exchanges between
individuals and the relationship between organisational processes and their intended out-
comes [9, 21]. Research on cyber threat intelligence sharing likewise identifies organisa-
tional and trust-related obstacles that extend beyond technical compatibility [1]. These


                                                                                        1
perspectives motivate an approach that treats the contribution and maintenance of knowl-
edge as part of the incident-response problem, alongside the ability to retrieve it.

1.2. Research problem and approach

The problem addressed is the gap between retaining cybersecurity information and making
it usable when a response decision is needed. Relevant knowledge may be dispersed across
documents written for different audiences, expressed in different terms or detached from
the experience that explains it. A response procedure can therefore be available without
its relevance being apparent. The research concerns how a knowledge-management system
can help analysts connect an incident question to applicable knowledge and understand
the basis for using it.
     Knowledge maintenance is central to this problem. Advice may become inappropriate
as systems, threats or organisational procedures change. NIST notes that the diversity
and changing detail of incident response make a single static publication insuﬀicient for
maintaining operational guidance [13]. In this study, we therefore treat currency and
context as conditions of useful knowledge reuse. We also include provenance, meaning
the identifiable origin and supporting basis of the knowledge presented, and confidentiality,
meaning that knowledge is available only to its authorised audience.
     We investigate machine-learning models trained on language data as a means of re-
lating an analyst’s question to relevant material and producing explanations informed by
that material. Research on retrieval-supported language generation provides a basis for
combining learned language capabilities with external knowledge sources [10]. We adopt
this as a research direction for making documented experience accessible in the context of
an analyst’s question. The question is whether this support helps people reach justified
decisions with less effort, while retaining the information needed to assess the guidance.
     This approach connects knowledge management with decision support. A useful ex-
planation needs to preserve the conditions under which advice applies and distinguish
supported recommendations from uncertainty. Analysts retain responsibility for assess-
ing operational consequences and choosing an action. Reliability and confidentiality are
consequently requirements for knowledge reuse, considered together with relevance and
ease of use.

1.3. Objectives and research questions

The overall objective is to design, implement and evaluate a knowledge-management sys-
tem (KMS) that uses machine learning to optimise knowledge reuse during cybersecurity
incident response. We define optimisation in practical terms: reducing the time and effort
needed to locate applicable knowledge and helping analysts make correct, well-supported
decisions. This definition gives decision quality equal importance to speed.
    The specific objectives are to identify the knowledge and maintenance practices needed


2
for incident-response tasks; to develop an approach that connects that knowledge with
relevant, traceable guidance; and to evaluate its performance and usability within the
conditions governing access to the knowledge. These objectives lead to the principal
research question:

      How can a knowledge-management system use machine learning to support
      faster, better-informed incident-response decisions while preserving the relia-
      bility and confidentiality of the knowledge used?

    Three research questions organise the investigation:

RQ1: What knowledge must be captured, organised and maintained to support incident-
     response decisions?

RQ2: How can machine-learning-based retrieval and language generation turn that knowl-
     edge into relevant, traceable guidance?

RQ3: How does the resulting system perform in retrieval, response quality, task eﬀiciency
     and usability, subject to access and provenance controls?

   Together, these questions connect the knowledge-management requirements to the
proposed support for analysts and to its evaluation. They keep the research focused on
the use of knowledge in a response task rather than on language generation in isolation.

1.4. Research methodology

We adopt Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM), which organises research
around the creation and evaluation of an artefact intended to address a practical
problem. Peffers et al. define six activities: problem identification and motivation,
definition of solution objectives, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and
communication [15]. This approach suits the investigation because the KMS provides
a means of examining how knowledge-management requirements and machine-learning
support work together in incident-response tasks.
    Problem identification began with a literature review of incident response and knowl-
edge sharing. We then conducted semi-structured interviews with a research specialist to
identify use cases, user roles and system requirements. This qualitative input informed
the definition of solution objectives and the knowledge-management requirements exam-
ined under RQ1. Design and development address those objectives through the research
artefact; Chapter 3 explains the design choices and their relationship to the problem.
    Demonstration concerns how the approach applies to incident-response situations.
Evaluation examines how well it meets the stated objectives [15]. We use this distinc-
tion to connect examples of knowledge reuse with a separate assessment of performance



                                                                                        3
and usefulness. Communication brings the research argument, design rationale, meth-
ods and findings together in this dissertation so that readers can assess the relationship
between the problem and the conclusions.
    The evaluation framework combines model-oriented and human-centred perspectives.
Retrieval quality concerns whether the material found is relevant and suﬀicient for the
question. Response quality concerns correctness, completeness and support for the ex-
planation in its sources. Task eﬀiciency concerns the time and effort required to reach a
justified response decision, while usability concerns how readily people can find, interpret
and assess the guidance. Access restrictions and provenance are conditions across these
dimensions: relevant material must be appropriate for the intended reader, and the basis
of advice must remain inspectable.
    These dimensions are considered together when interpreting performance. Task com-
pletion time must be interpreted alongside the correctness and support of the resulting
decision. Similarly, a response that reads clearly still requires assessment of its correctness
and applicability. The evaluation therefore connects properties of retrieval and generated
guidance with the analyst’s use of that guidance, rather than treating either perspective
as suﬀicient on its own. Chapter 4 presents the methods, results and interpretation.

1.5. Scope and contribution

The scope is knowledge-based decision support for cybersecurity incident response. It
includes the use of procedures, threat-related documentation and recorded incident expe-
rience to inform an analyst’s understanding of a situation. It also includes the capture
and maintenance needed to make that material reusable. The intended users are analysts
consulting knowledge and the contributors responsible for keeping it relevant.
    The study focuses on assistance with interpretation and response planning. Au-
tonomous remediation and the replacement of professional judgement fall outside its
scope. The contribution is an approach linking incident-response knowledge requirements
to machine-learning-supported guidance, together with an evaluation of that approach.
Its interpretation is tied to the knowledge, tasks and participants considered in the study,
providing a basis for discussing where the approach is useful and what limits its applica-
tion.

1.6. Dissertation organisation

Chapter 2 establishes the conceptual background through incident response, knowledge
management and machine learning. Chapter 3 presents the KMS requirements, design
and development.
    Chapter 4 examines the evaluation dimensions and findings. Chapter 5 discusses
contributions, limitations and further work. Supporting scenarios and evaluation-record
definitions appear in the appendices.

                                  State of the Art

An incident-response decision requires knowledge that applies to the affected environment,
supports a defensible course of action, and remains available for examination. We review
organisational knowledge sharing, machine-learning representations, retrieval-augmented
generation, and evaluation from this perspective. The chapter offers a focused narrative
synthesis rather than an exhaustive systematic review. Its comparison of cybersecurity
studies considers the task, knowledge sources, model adaptation, and evaluation method,
since improvements on different tasks cannot be interpreted as a common measure of
incident-response effectiveness.

2.1. Incident response and organisational knowledge

NIST Special Publication 800-61r3 places incident response within continuing cybersecu-
rity risk management. In its Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 model, Detect, Respond,
and Recover cover incident handling, while Govern, Identify, and Protect support prepa-
ration and risk reduction. Lessons learned inform improvements across these activities
rather than belonging only to a final reporting stage. NIST also distinguishes an observ-
able event from an incident: interpreting evidence is necessary to establish whether an
incident has occurred [13]. This distinction matters for knowledge support. Retrieving a
description of an attack does not establish that the attack explains an observed event.
    Consider an analyst investigating suspected account compromise. A threat report
can explain a technique, an internal procedure can specify an approved response, and
a previous investigation can document exceptions encountered in a similar environment.
The analyst still needs to assess which observations support the suspicion and whether
the procedure applies. This example separates case evidence from reusable knowledge.
Event records describe what was observed; response knowledge supplies the concepts,
procedures, and prior experience needed to interpret those records. Their connection is a
judgement about applicability, not simply a match between keywords.
    We use knowledge-management system (KMS) to encompass the organisational prac-
tices and information services through which knowledge is captured, organised, main-
tained, shared, and reused. Ipe’s conceptual framework locates knowledge sharing in
interactions between individuals, while Yeboah distinguishes its enablers, processes, and
outcomes [9, 21]. These perspectives explain why a searchable collection alone is insuﬀi-
cient: useful experience must first become accessible to others, and its reuse must serve an


                                                                                          5
organisational objective. For incident response, that objective is to help people reach cor-
rect, well-supported decisions with less effort. Faster production of an answer is valuable
only when its interpretation and use remain appropriate.

2.2. The incident-response knowledge lifecycle

Knowledge capture concerns both formal guidance and experience gained during investi-
gations. NIST recommends documented procedures, including playbooks that organise
actionable tasks for particular situations, and the incorporation of lessons learned into
improved practice [13]. A useful incident lesson records more than the final action: it
explains the conditions under which the action was chosen and what evidence justified it.
Otherwise, later responders may copy an outcome without understanding its constraints.
The distinction between recording an incident and producing reusable guidance follows
from the knowledge-sharing emphasis on creating, sharing, and applying knowledge [9].
    Organisation makes those conditions discoverable. Osman et al. compare knowledge-
management tools through dimensions including knowledge sources, extraction, input,
retrieval, and sharing, placing search within a broader process [14]. Applied to incident
response, this perspective suggests describing knowledge by its purpose and context: af-
fected technology, incident type, intended audience, author or responsible team, and date
of applicability. A vendor advisory and an internal recovery procedure may concern the
same vulnerability while answering different questions. Relevance therefore includes what
a document enables the responder to do, not only whether it discusses the same topic.
    Shared vocabularies can reduce ambiguity when knowledge crosses organisational
boundaries. Takahashi and Kadobayashi’s reference ontology coordinates cybersecurity
operational information and existing information schemas [20]. An ontology defines con-
cepts and their relationships; a knowledge graph represents particular entities and rela-
tions using such a vocabulary. These approaches can connect an attack technique to
affected assets or mitigations, but structured representation does not establish that a
relationship is correct. Li et al. identify noise and conflict during graph construction
as sources of misleading inference [11]. For knowledge reuse, consistent terminology and
reliable source support matter before more elaborate reasoning becomes useful.
    Maintenance addresses changes in both the environment and the knowledge itself.
Dhingra et al. demonstrate the diﬀiculty of representing time-sensitive facts in language
models trained on data snapshots [5]. The corresponding KMS problem is broader than
model freshness: a formerly valid procedure can become unsuitable after a platform
change, and an incident hypothesis can be corrected after further investigation. Current
operational advice and a historical explanation may therefore require different versions of a
source. Recording validity periods, review responsibility, and superseded guidance allows
those uses to remain distinguishable rather than treating the newest text as appropriate
for every question.
    Sharing also depends on trust and permitted disclosure. Abraham et al.’s interview-


6
informed perspective on cyber threat intelligence (CTI) stresses human interpretation, in-
compatible platforms, and different meanings of trust [1]. A relevant internal lesson may
contain information that cannot be shared with another team or organisation. Knowledge
quality, topical relevance, and permission to use the material are separate considerations.
The lifecycle closes when responders can report an incorrect or incomplete recommenda-
tion and responsible contributors can revise the underlying knowledge. This feedback
connects retrieval to organisational learning instead of allowing errors to recur in later
answers.

2.3. Machine learning for retrieval and generation

2.3.1. Learned representations and model adaptation

Machine learning can support knowledge access without requiring a new incident classifier.
A classifier assigns an input to a defined category, such as malicious or benign, whereas
a representation model produces features used to compare inputs. A large language
model (LLM) used for generation produces text conditioned on its input. These roles can
coexist, and the task differs from the method used to train the model. Sentence-BERT, for
example, learns representations through fine-tuning but subsequently uses the resulting
embeddings for similarity comparisons [16].
    An embedding is a numerical representation of text. Sentence-BERT adapts a pre-
trained language model so that sentences can be encoded separately and compared using
measures such as cosine similarity. This permits document representations to be com-
puted before a query arrives, avoiding a separate joint model evaluation for every possible
sentence pair [16]. In an incident-response setting, semantic retrieval may connect a de-
scription of suspicious account activity with guidance phrased in different terminology.
Similarity remains a retrieval signal, however: it does not establish that the guidance is
current, authorised, or correct for the affected system.
    Pretraining learns parameters from earlier data; inference uses those parameters to
process new inputs. Using a pretrained embedding model is therefore an application of
machine learning even when no local training occurs. Fine-tuning updates model parame-
ters for a task or domain. Retrieval instead supplies selected external material at inference
time, and few-shot prompting supplies examples in the input without itself changing the
parameters. These mechanisms are compatible. Lewis et al.’s original retrieval-augmented
generation approach combines pretrained retrieval and generation with task-specific fine-
tuning; retrieval should not be defined as the absence of training [10].

2.3.2. Retrieval and source-grounded generation

Lexical and semantic retrieval offer different routes to relevant knowledge. Exact terms
matter for identifiers, product names, and error messages, while learned representations
can recover material with related meaning. Hybrid retrieval combines these signals. A


                                                                                           7
reranker then reorders an initial candidate set using a further relevance assessment. RAG-
Intel illustrates this sequence through keyword and dense retrieval followed by reranking
and context reduction [2]. The attraction for incident response is practical: precise tech-
nical references and loosely described symptoms can occur in the same query. Whether
combining retrieval methods improves a particular task remains an empirical question.
    Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) conditions a language model on external in-
formation selected for the current request. Lewis et al. distinguish knowledge encoded in
model parameters from knowledge held in a retrievable collection. Their model combines
a neural retriever with a sequence-to-sequence generator and evaluates the combination on
knowledge-intensive language tasks [10]. This separation provides a route for introducing
revised knowledge through the collection rather than relying entirely on model retraining.
For a KMS, the intended benefit is an explanation grounded in applicable sources, while
the original documents remain available for inspection.
    The unit of retrieval affects what evidence reaches the generator. Splitting a docu-
ment into passages, often called chunks, permits focused retrieval but can separate an
instruction from its preconditions. Retrieving an entire document preserves more con-
text at the cost of additional irrelevant material. RAGAs treats context relevance and
answer faithfulness as separate concerns because finding useful information and using it
correctly are different problems [6]. A citation consequently needs more than a matching
document title: the cited passage must support the associated statement, including any
qualifications that change its operational meaning.

2.4. Related cybersecurity systems

MoRSE addresses broad cybersecurity question answering through specialised retrievers
over structured and unstructured knowledge. Its evaluation covers 600 questions con-
cerning general cybersecurity, multi-document reasoning, and vulnerabilities, with expert-
validated reference answers and model-assisted assessment. The study reports advantages
over its selected language-model comparators [19]. Its contribution is evidence that or-
ganising heterogeneous cybersecurity sources around a query can improve answers in that
benchmark. The evaluation unit remains the answer, however, rather than an analyst’s
completed incident-response task. The result therefore supports investigation of retrieval
mechanisms without establishing how much time a responder would save.
    GenDFIR concentrates on incident timeline analysis. It converts event data into a
structured textual knowledge base, retrieves relevant events, and uses a language model
without task-specific fine-tuning to produce an interpreted timeline. Its evaluation uses
synthetic cyber incidents and task-specific assessments of evidence retrieval and resulting
analysis [12]. Unlike a reference-document assistant, it retrieves observations belonging
to the investigation itself. This makes event coverage and chronological relationships
central to the task. Controlled scenarios allow the expected evidence to be specified, but
performance on them leaves uncertainty about incomplete, inconsistent, or unfamiliar


8
evidence in operational investigations.
    RAGIntel uses MITRE ATT&CK knowledge for attack-investigation questions and re-
ports performance comparable to standalone language models in its evaluation [2]. This
is a useful qualification to MoRSE’s positive comparison: a retrieval stage can provide
an explicit knowledge source without necessarily improving aggregate answer scores. Dif-
ferences in source coverage, question type, and generator make the studies informative
comparisons of approaches, not a ranking of systems under common conditions.
    ForensicLLM investigates local model adaptation for digital-forensics questions. It
fine-tunes a model with reduced-precision weights using question–answer material derived
from research articles and curated digital artefacts. The study compares the resulting
configuration with a base model and a retrieval-augmented baseline, reporting stronger
correctness and relevance alongside professional assessments of the responses [18]. This
comparison shifts attention from the retrieval mechanism alone to the complete model
configuration and its domain preparation. Its local deployment also raises a distinct
consideration: the feasibility of using specialist assistance where forensic information
cannot be sent to an external generation service.

2.5. Reliability, confidentiality, and adversarial content

Retrieval introduces evidence into generation, but it also introduces an attack surface.
Greshake et al. demonstrate indirect prompt injection, in which attacker-controlled con-
tent encountered by an LLM-integrated application contains instructions intended to
redirect its behaviour. Their experiments include attempts to manipulate outputs and
disclose information [7]. A cybersecurity report can therefore be useful evidence while
also carrying hostile instructions. Source content needs to be treated as material to as-
sess, not as authority to redefine the task or grant access. Separating instructions from
evidence is a necessary defensive objective, but formatting or prompt wording alone does
not establish resistance to these attacks.
    Ordinary knowledge errors create a related reliability problem. An answer can repro-
duce an outdated source faithfully, omit a procedural exception, or express an uncertain
incident hypothesis as fact. The Retrieval-Augmented Generation Benchmark (RGB)
tests noise robustness, rejection when evidence is insuﬀicient, integration of information,
and robustness to false information; the evaluated models show particular diﬀiculties with
the latter three abilities [3]. For incident guidance, uncertainty and conflicting evidence
therefore deserve explicit treatment rather than being hidden behind a single fluent rec-
ommendation.
    Confidentiality concerns the complete information flow. Restricting document access
is ineffective if restricted content can still reach an unauthorised answer, and a local
generator does not by itself determine where embedding, retrieval, or evaluation services
process data. Combining the trust concerns identified in CTI sharing with the disclosure
risks demonstrated by prompt injection suggests treating authorisation as a constraint
on eligible evidence, not a preference inferred by a language model [1, 7]. Source trace-
ability then operates within that constraint: an explanation should expose the evidence
a responder is permitted to inspect without disclosing protected material.

2.6. Evaluating knowledge access and response decisions

A KMS evaluation needs to distinguish finding knowledge, explaining it, and using it in
a decision. At the retrieval level, precision measures the proportion of returned material
judged relevant, while recall measures how much of the annotated relevant material was
recovered. Rank matters because evidence placed far down a result list may be costly to
locate. Passage-level assessment also differs from document-level relevance: a document
can concern the right incident type while the selected passage omits the required proce-
dure. For access-controlled collections, the relevant set must be defined for the requesting
identity and the temporal scope of the question.
    RAGAs separates context relevance, answer relevance, and faithfulness. Its faithful-
ness method decomposes a response into statements and uses a language model to assess
whether the context supports them [6]. ARES trains lightweight judges for related dimen-


10
sions and combines their predictions with human annotations through prediction-powered
inference to estimate system performance with confidence intervals [17]. These methods
support scalable comparison, but their agreement with expert judgements needs checking
in the target domain. Faithfulness to an incorrect source is not factual correctness, and
factual correctness alone does not establish that a proposed action is suitable for a par-
ticular environment. Citation support and operational applicability consequently require
separate examination.
    Negative cases help reveal failures obscured by answerable questions. Following the
distinctions examined in RGB, an assessment can include irrelevant passages, contradic-
tory evidence, and requests for which the accessible collection contains no answer [3].
Appropriate abstention means recognising insuﬀicient evidence; an unnecessary refusal
when relevant evidence is available instead indicates a failure of retrieval or generation.
Temporal cases add another distinction: a historical account may correctly cite super-
seded guidance, whereas a request for current response advice requires currently applica-
ble knowledge. These cases test the knowledge lifecycle as well as the model’s language
behaviour.
    For human outcomes, response quality and task eﬀiciency need joint measurement.
A search-only condition can establish how analysts perform when they inspect sources
directly. Generation without retrieval tests the contribution of model knowledge, while
generation with retrieval tests the additional value of contextual evidence. Comparisons
are interpretable when the cases, available knowledge, and model conditions are con-
trolled. Elapsed time should cover the response task rather than only model latency.
Independently assessed decision correctness, severity of errors, and source consultation
help distinguish useful acceleration from faster acceptance of unsupported advice. Usabil-
ity and confidence capture the responder’s experience but do not replace those outcome
measures.
    The organisational perspective extends evaluation beyond an individual answer.
Yeboah’s separation of knowledge-sharing processes and outcomes suggests examining
whether new lessons are contributed and subsequently reused [21]. Relevant KMS
measures include the time required to make approved knowledge available, whether
corrections displace obsolete advice, and whether contributors can identify material
needing review. A system that answers a fixed benchmark well may still lose value as its
collection changes. Evaluation therefore benefits from both controlled retrieval tasks and
observation of how knowledge remains usable through contribution, revision, and reuse.

2.7. Synthesis and research gap

The reviewed studies connect incident-response knowledge to several complementary re-
search problems. Knowledge-sharing research explains why capture and maintenance de-
pend on people and organisational purpose. Learned representations support access across
differences in wording, while RAG offers a way to condition explanations on retrievable


                                                                                        11
sources. Cybersecurity evaluations establish task-specific benefits and limitations, with
no uniform advantage for retrieval across the compared settings [2, 8, 9, 19].
    The remaining question is how these mechanisms support the complete path from
maintained knowledge to a defensible incident-response decision. We therefore frame RQ1
around the knowledge and lifecycle needed for reuse, RQ2 around relevant retrieval and
traceable guidance, and RQ3 around retrieval quality, response quality, task eﬀiciency, and
usability under confidentiality constraints. This framing treats optimisation as reduced
effort accompanied by sounder decisions, not as output speed alone. Chapter 3 develops
the design from these requirements.

3.1. Design purpose and use cases

We developed Sentinel as a knowledge-management system that uses machine learning
to support incident analysis and response. It brings together a document library, source
ingestion, and a conversational assistant. The purpose is to make applicable knowledge
easier to locate, interpret, and reuse when an analyst must decide what to investigate
or do next. Embedding models help retrieve relevant passages; a language model uses
those passages to explain response options. Publication controls and inspectable evidence
connect this assistance to maintained organisational knowledge.
    Consider an analyst receiving the report, “I entered my password after following a sus-
picious message.” The useful knowledge may be distributed across a phishing playbook,
account-recovery guidance, and a local procedure assigning responsibility for identity ad-
ministration. Sentinel provides two entry points: the analyst can browse the library
directly or describe the situation to the assistant. The assistant receives the observations,
retrieves accessible material, and is instructed to distinguish reported facts from possible
explanations. The analyst can then open the supporting passages before deciding whether
the advice fits the affected service and their authority to act.
    This example defines the system’s advisory scope. Sentinel supports the interpretation
of incident information and the preparation of a response; the responder remains respon-
sible for decisions and changes to operational systems. Knowledge custodians maintain
the material on which that assistance depends. Editors propose revisions, administrators
control publication, and operators monitor ingestion and service availability. Together,
these roles connect knowledge capture and maintenance in RQ1 to contextual, traceable
guidance in RQ2. RQ3 concerns how well that design supports retrieval, response quality,
task eﬀiciency, and usability.

3.3. Architecture and allocation of responsibilities

Sentinel uses a React interface, a Spring application, and PostgreSQL with pgvector
for vector storage. Figure 3.1 shows how these parts connect. The interface presents
knowledge and captures requests. The application coordinates publication, ingestion,
retrieval, and generation. PostgreSQL retains the knowledge, access rules, processing
state, and evidence references needed by those activities. Identity and model services
remain separately configurable dependencies.


14
    We place publication rules and assistant orchestration in the same application so that
a retrieved passage follows the same access policy as an article opened in the library.
Keeping relational and vector data together also allows retrieval to check publication
and membership alongside candidate selection. This choice reduces the number of ser-
vices needed to coordinate evidence eligibility. Its cost is tighter coupling between search
and the knowledge schema: changes to classification or lifecycle rules must be reflected
consistently in direct reading and retrieval.
    The data model separates representations with different purposes. An article provides
the current reading view. A revision preserves a particular content state. An evidence
snapshot retains the text against which a citation is resolved. Embeddings are derived
search representations and can be regenerated without redefining the underlying knowl-
edge. This separation allows an editor to revise guidance while preserving the identity of
material previously used in a conversation. A separate assertion store records evidence-
linked entities and relationships; the assistant retrieves passages rather than traversing
that store.
    Long-running work is handled asynchronously. Admission records a source before
an ingestion worker extracts and indexes it. Publication records a reindex request in
the same transaction as the knowledge change. The browser can therefore distinguish
acceptance from subsequent processing, while a restart can recover outstanding work. We
accept a delay between publication and search availability in exchange for keeping model
calls outside the publication transaction and protecting the identity of the revision being
indexed.

3.4. Knowledge organisation and maintenance

3.4.1. Articles and curated incident playbooks

The library organises articles by category, severity, tags, source, and update date. Arti-
cle bodies contain titled sections, with related documents and indicators of compromise
where applicable. These fields serve different reading tasks: a category narrows the type
of knowledge, a source identifies its origin, and related documents connect an initial ob-
servation to further investigation. Severity describes the material’s assigned importance;
it does not diagnose the incident described by an analyst.
    We include a curated collection of response playbooks to give the assistant procedural
material alongside advisories and technical descriptions. Its scenarios include phishing,
ransomware, account takeover, exposed credentials, application compromise, data expo-
sure, and recovery. Each playbook follows a recognisable structure: applicability, ques-
tions to answer, first actions, evidence to preserve, containment and recovery, verification,
and escalation. Decision checkpoints explain where context changes the response. For ex-
ample, the phishing guidance distinguishes receiving a message from entering credentials
or executing a downloaded file. We include these distinctions so that retrieved material
can address the reported interaction rather than describe the threat only.
    The playbooks are source-attributed editorial syntheses, identified as AI-assisted and
requiring human review. Their admission checks require attribution, declared categories,
consistent identifiers, and the review notice; they also exclude unverified indicators from
this general guidance. Installation adds missing articles through the governed publication
process and preserves existing content when it differs. A custodian must review such differ-
ences rather than lose local adaptations during a refresh. Here, approval permits reference
use; operational applicability still depends on review in the receiving organisation.

3.4.2. Review, publication, and reuse

An editor can create a draft revision and submit it for review while the current approved
article remains available. Administrative approval rejects self-approval and publishes the
accepted revision. Rejection leaves the existing publication intact. State-version checks
detect a decision based on an outdated workflow state, which matters when several custodi-
ans are maintaining the same procedure. This workflow supports knowledge improvement
without making unfinished advice available to the assistant.
    Supersession publishes a reviewed successor, whereas administrative rollback creates
a new revision from previously published content. Rollback preserves a forward-moving
revision history and uses a separate administrative publication route. Expiry removes
material from present read eligibility while retaining its history. These operations make
maintenance part of knowledge reuse: a response procedure can remain attributable with-
out remaining suitable for current advice.
    The publication transaction also records durable reindex work. Replacement is tied


16
to the intended revision and governance state, so a delayed worker cannot publish search
material for an obsolete state. During reindexing, an approved revision may temporarily
lack searchable embeddings. We favour this gap over allowing superseded guidance to
enter a new answer simply because its vectors are still stored.
    We keep saved answers separate from published guidance. A conversation can support
follow-up work, while knowledge custodians decide which lessons belong in a revised article.
This separation prevents a generated response from acquiring publication authority merely
because it has been retained. Reuse therefore depends on editorial judgement as well as
the ability to search prior knowledge.

3.4.3. Maintaining externally supplied knowledge

Source connectors complement local material with vulnerability and incident informa-
tion. Refreshes for NVD, CISA KEV, and SANS ISC use persisted work queues, progress
records, and source checkpoints. Unchanged records retain their revision; changed source-
owned records receive new revisions and evidence. Manual policy or content changes
require review instead of being silently overwritten by a feed. Fetching and reindexing
remain separate stages, so connector progress and search readiness answer different oper-
ational questions.
    Trusted producers can publish reference material through an explicit admission route
without manual review of each item. This is a decision about which producers the KMS
accepts, with the corresponding maintenance responsibility assigned to its operators. To-
gether with the curated collection and editor-managed articles, it gives the library several
acquisition routes while retaining a common publication and evidence model.

3.5. Recoverable ingestion of local and remote sources

Incident knowledge also arrives as uploaded documents or material imported from a URL.
Sentinel checks uploads for accepted formats and size, assigns source identities, and re-
tains their content before exposing a queued job. URL admission checks the destination;
the worker retains the first successful response before extraction. A retry consequently
processes the same retained material rather than relying on an open browser session or
downloading an unnoticed replacement. Optional owner-scoped request identifiers allow
repeated submissions of the same request to recover its existing job.
    Processing separates extraction, sanitisation, chunking, embedding, and indexing. For
uploaded text, the configured chunker uses character windows of 1200 characters with an
overlap of 150. Overlap retains some context across boundaries, while fixed windows
keep processing predictable across document formats. The tradeoff is that a window
may divide a sentence or procedure. Governed articles instead provide retained evidence
passages with explicit identities. Missing source content or empty extraction produces a
failed job that can be inspected and, when appropriate, retried.


                                                                                         17
    A worker holds a time-limited claim with a token and processing generation. Progress
updates and index replacement must match that live claim. Recovery advances the gen-
eration, preventing a delayed worker from overwriting a later retry or restoring deleted
material. Repeated embedding calls remain possible when a worker loses its claim after
contacting a provider; the protected operation is the replacement of searchable content.
This design addresses F2 by preserving continuity of knowledge acquisition through inter-
ruptions.
    An indexed upload retains its own access scope and processing history. Indexing
alone does not turn it into an approved library article. The assistant can use an au-
thorised upload as grounding, but article publication and article-level citations require
their corresponding evidence identities. Keeping these routes distinct allows an analyst
to consult submitted material without making it organisational guidance by accident.

3.6. Machine-learning retrieval and incident guidance

The assistant follows retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): it retrieves knowledge for
the reported situation and supplies that material to a language model. We use machine
learning for representation and explanation, while explicit application rules determine
which knowledge is eligible and how its use can be inspected.

3.6.1. Combining semantic and lexical retrieval

Machine learning enters retrieval through embeddings: numerical representations of pas-
sages and questions produced by an embedding model. Sentinel compares the question
representation with stored passage vectors using cosine distance. We use this route to
address differences between the language of a report and the terminology of the library. A
person may describe encrypted files without naming the relevant response category. Lexi-
cal search supplies a complementary route for terms and identifiers that appear explicitly
in the question and stored text.
    Both searches apply article eligibility and access conditions before limiting their can-
didate lists. Eligible article passages belong to the current approved, active, unexpired
revision and match a retained evidence span. Upload candidates follow their durable-
source access rules. Filtering at this point allows accessible material to compete for the
available positions instead of first filling them with inaccessible matches. If vector retrieval
fails, lexical retrieval still applies the same access conditions.
    We combine the two ranked lists using reciprocal-rank fusion [4]. For passage d, the
ordering score is

3.6.2. Establishing the context for an answer

The assistant supplements hybrid retrieval with access-aware indicator lookup. This gives
literal technical observations a route to matching knowledge alongside the broader seman-
tic search. Indicator lookup uses the original question and is separate from the hybrid
search’s category and severity filters. Its evidence span identifies the indicator occurrence;
a surrounding summary still needs to be checked against the full article. After combining
candidates, the application resolves readable articles and exact evidence references before
constructing model context. An optional reranker receives only authorised candidates.
    Conversational rewriting can turn a follow-up question into a standalone search ques-
tion using recent turns. We place this step after literal-question retrieval has established
usable evidence. If that initial retrieval is empty, the assistant returns a local message di-
recting the analyst to refine the question or browse playbooks. This conservative ordering
avoids a generated answer detached from accessible knowledge, although it can also leave a
context-dependent follow-up unanswered. The question may already have reached the em-
bedding provider during retrieval; the gate controls the subsequent rewriting, reranking,
and answer-generation stages.
    The prompt combines the question, bounded recent conversation, and selected sources.
Each article passage carries source information and a server-issued evidence token. The
source-context budget limits prompt growth, at the cost of potentially truncating the
final passage. We therefore treat retained grounding references as the material selected
for context, rather than a record of the model’s reasoning. Knowledge remains external
to the generation model: revisions can change what is retrieved without retraining it.

3.6.3. Turning retrieved knowledge into response guidance

We shape the generation instructions around the decisions an incident responder faces. For
suspected incidents, the model is asked to separate observations from explanations and
organise supported advice around immediate actions, investigation and evidence preserva-
tion, recovery, and verification. The instructions call for an explanation of each action’s
purpose, expected result, and responsible actor. They limit clarification to three focused
questions and prohibit assumptions about the operating system, affected platform, or
responder’s administrative access.
    The instructions also require the model to explain operational impact before disruptive
actions and to preserve evidence before cleanup. Requests for redacted observations re-
place requests for passwords, secret keys, verification codes, or complete sensitive logs. A
conceptual question can receive a short explanation instead of a forced incident checklist.

These instructions define the intended assistance; the reviewable sources and subsequent
answer assessment remain necessary because a language model can misapply them.
    The application supports structured answer blocks and a natural-language conversa-
tion route. Both use the same practical guidance and authorised context. The model is
instructed to copy evidence tokens supplied by the server. Response processing resolves
accepted tokens to retained references and removes unsupported citation tokens. Arti-
cle citations and exact-passage references are then associated with the assistant message,
making the advice available for later inspection.

3.6.4. Useful behaviour when generation is unavailable

A model failure need not remove access to existing guidance. When generation fails or is
temporarily blocked, Sentinel first looks for an eligible playbook among the retrieved arti-
cles. It independently resolves up to three bounded sections titled First actions, Questions
to answer, and Evidence to preserve from that playbook’s current approved revision. The
section text comes from retained evidence, with a separate access check for each passage,
rather than from trusting the retrieval metadata alone.
    The fallback quotes these sections and labels them as existing playbook guidance, not
a personalised answer. If section expansion cannot be authorised, it provides available
retrieved article references for direct reading. It does not combine fragments into a locally
invented response plan. This behaviour preserves a useful part of the KMS during a
generation outage while making the loss of contextual adaptation visible. The same final
authorisation and citation-persistence checks apply to fallback and generated responses.

immediate provider-token display.

Access policy supports knowledge sharing at different levels. Public articles require au-

material require editor and administrator authority respectively. Active database member-
ships determine those roles. Missing or inconsistent governance results in denial. These
controls apply to direct reading, retrieval, and evidence disclosure, so a conversational
request does not provide an alternative route around the library’s restrictions.
    Retrieved content is treated as untrusted data. Sanitisation targets recognisable in-
jection patterns before uploaded text is indexed and before source text enters a prompt.
The prompt distinguishes source material from instructions. These defences reduce expo-
sure to known patterns but require adversarial evaluation, particularly when unfamiliar
documents are admitted. The separation of source permissions from text and metadata
remains necessary even when sanitisation leaves a passage apparently harmless.
    For N2, revision and workflow histories describe knowledge changes, while job records
explain processing progress. Redacted audit events record consequential operations and
outcomes. Successful transactional changes produce their success events after the trans-
action completes. Audit persistence is best-effort: an audit failure is logged without
replacing the original operation’s result. This favours service continuity but leaves a com-
pleteness limitation that operators must consider when investigating an incident involving
the KMS itself.
    For N3, chat and embedding services can use hosted providers or local models through
Ollama. Database and identity hosting are configured separately. Choosing local infer-
ence therefore addresses model processing, while the complete disclosure boundary also
depends on where knowledge and identity services run. Hosted embedding requests trans-
mit passage or question text; hosted answer requests transmit the question, selected
context, and recent conversation. Access eligibility within the KMS and permission to
send material to a provider are separate deployment considerations.
    The vector store expects 1024-dimensional embeddings. Replacing the embedding
model requires reindexing; a different dimensionality also requires a storage change. Re-
producibility and confidentiality depend on these deployment choices, not only on the
chat model.

3.9. Chapter synthesis

Sentinel connects knowledge acquisition, retrieval and response guidance. Playbooks sup-
ply procedures; embeddings and lexical search locate passages; generation relates them to
the incident. Direct reading and fallback preserve access when generation fails. Chapter 4
examines this design against its requirements, distinguishing technical operation from the
quality and usefulness of the assistance.

4.1. Evaluation approach

The evaluation examines whether Sentinel connects maintained knowledge to useful sup-
port for incident-response decisions. Following Chapter 1, we interpret optimisation as
reducing the time and effort needed to locate applicable knowledge while supporting cor-
rect, well-founded decisions. A faster answer is therefore not suﬀicient: the retrieved
material must address the situation, the explanation must preserve its meaning, and the
analyst must be able to assess its applicability.
    We completed model comparisons and human evaluation as part of the research, along-
side engineering verification. We distinguish these activities by their object of assessment.
Software checks examine specified application behaviour. Live provider calls establish
compatibility with particular services. Comparative model results concern retrieval and
generated responses, while human evaluation connects the assistance to its use and inter-
pretation. These perspectives contribute to the same research objective without sharing
a common unit of observation.
    We use retrieval quality, response quality, task eﬀiciency, and usability as analytical
dimensions. Access restrictions and provenance apply across them: useful knowledge must
be available to the intended reader, and the basis of advice must remain inspectable. The
following definitions establish the meaning of these dimensions before the technical results
and their relationship to RQ1–RQ3 are discussed.

4.2. Evaluation dimensions

4.2.1. Retrieval quality

Retrieval quality concerns whether the returned knowledge helps answer the incident
question. Topical similarity is only one part of relevance. A passage about account com-
promise may describe the correct threat but omit the recovery procedure or the conditions
governing its use. Article-level relevance identifies a useful document; passage-level rel-
evance asks whether the selected text contains the information needed for the decision.
We distinguish these units because an appropriate article can still yield an incomplete
context for generation.
   Precision describes the proportion of retrieved items judged relevant, whereas recall
describes the proportion of the relevant set recovered. A ranking cutoff limits the results


                                                                                          25
considered. Mean reciprocal rank summarises the position of the first relevant result, and
normalised discounted cumulative gain assesses the ordering of relevant material against
an ideal ranking. These measures distinguish finding some useful evidence from recovering
the evidence needed to address the whole question.
    The reference set also depends on access and temporal scope. Material outside the
requesting identity’s permissions is not an eligible target, and superseded guidance may be
relevant to a historical question without being appropriate for current advice. Questions
with no relevant accessible evidence form a distinct category; recall has no meaningful
denominator for an empty reference set. Returning unrelated material in that situation
is different from failing to retrieve an available answer.

4.2.2. Response quality and traceability

Response quality concerns how the assistant uses knowledge, not merely whether it pro-
duces fluent text. RAGAs distinguishes retrieval of useful context, faithful use of that
context, and generation quality. ARES similarly separates context relevance, answer
faithfulness, and answer relevance [6, 17]. We adopt this separation as an analytical basis
for examining guidance without treating any one dimension as a complete measure of
correctness.
    Relevance concerns whether the response addresses the question. Faithfulness con-
cerns whether its claims are supported by the supplied material. Correctness additionally
concerns whether the advice is accurate and applicable to the stated situation. Com-
pleteness concerns the information necessary to make the decision, including important
qualifications and dependencies. For example, quoting a recovery action faithfully does
not make the advice complete if the source also requires evidence preservation before that
action. A response can thus be well grounded and still omit something consequential.
    Traceability makes the supporting material available for examination. Citation reso-
lution establishes that a reference identifies an inspectable source and passage. Citation
support concerns whether that passage justifies the associated statement; citation cover-
age concerns how much of the evidence-requiring advice has adequate support. Sentinel’s
retained passage identities address the first problem, while the meaning of a claim–passage
relationship remains a quality question.
    Uncertainty is part of this assessment. A useful response distinguishes observed facts
from hypotheses and recognises insuﬀicient evidence. Appropriate abstention avoids un-
supported guidance; unnecessary abstention withholds assistance despite adequate evi-
dence. Likewise, error severity matters: an imprecise explanation and a recommendation
that risks destroying incident evidence have different consequences. These distinctions
connect language quality to the decisions the KMS is intended to support.




26
4.2.3. Task eﬀiciency and usability

Task eﬀiciency concerns the effort needed to reach a justified response decision. It includes
finding sources, reading them, checking the explanation, and deciding what applies. Model
latency measures only part of this work. Time to first visible output and time to a
complete answer also describe different experiences, particularly because Sentinel checks
the grounding before releasing a generated response.
    We interpret eﬀiciency together with decision quality. Rapid acceptance of unsup-
ported advice is not an improvement in incident response. Conversely, additional time
spent checking a critical passage may support a better decision. The relevant compari-
son is therefore between task outcomes and the effort associated with them, rather than
between response times alone.
    Usability concerns how readily people can navigate the knowledge, express their ques-
tion, understand the response, and inspect its basis. Perceived usefulness and confidence
describe the experience of using the system; they do not establish correctness. The abil-
ity to recognise an unavailable source or challenge a recommendation is also relevant to
usability because successful interaction includes knowing when further investigation is
necessary.

4.5. Model comparisons and human evaluation

4.5.1. Interpreting model comparisons

A model comparison describes performance under particular knowledge and configuration
conditions. The embedding model affects which material is retrieved, while the gener-
ation model affects how the supplied material is expressed. Corpus coverage, passage

28
boundaries, context limits, and access permissions also shape the available evidence. A
difference between complete configurations therefore cannot automatically be attributed
to one model or to retrieval alone.
    We distinguish retrieval outcomes from answer outcomes when interpreting compar-
ative performance. An improved ranking can make supporting knowledge easier to find
without ensuring that the answer uses it correctly. Similarly, a convincing answer may
rely on general model knowledge rather than the maintained collection. The relationship
between the question, retrieved passages, response, and quality judgement is consequently
more informative than a single aggregate score. Failed requests also belong to the inter-
pretation of a configuration’s practical usefulness, alongside the quality of its successful
responses.

4.5.2. Interpreting human evaluation

Human evaluation connects the system’s outputs to how people understand and use knowl-
edge. We distinguish assessments of an answer from assessments of interaction with the
KMS. Judging the clarity of a displayed recommendation concerns the response; locating
its source, checking applicability, and reaching a decision concern a broader task. This
distinction determines how far an observation can support a claim about incident-response
work.
    Quantitative outcomes and qualitative feedback also answer different questions. A
task-time observation describes elapsed work under defined conditions, while a comment
about saving time expresses perceived value. Feedback can explain diﬀiculties with ter-
minology, source navigation, or the amount of detail without establishing their frequency
across other users. Evidence consultation is similarly interpretable only in context: open-
ing a passage demonstrates access to it, not necessarily understanding or appropriate
reliance.

                                      CHAPTER 5

                                     Conclusion

This dissertation examined how a knowledge-management system can use machine learn-
ing to support cybersecurity incident-response decisions. We developed Sentinel to con-
nect maintained articles and response playbooks with contextual retrieval and source-
based explanations. The starting point was a practical knowledge-management problem:
an analyst needs the conditions and rationale behind a procedure, not only the document
containing it. We therefore treated capture, maintenance and reuse as parts of the same
task, with the analyst retaining responsibility for action. Optimisation means reducing
the effort needed to reach a correct, well-supported decision, rather than simply producing
an answer more quickly.

5.1. Answers to the research questions

For RQ1, our design answer is to retain procedures, threat-related material and incident
lessons with the context needed to judge their applicability. A playbook needs condi-
tions, responsibilities and verification steps; an incident lesson needs the observations
and reasoning behind an earlier decision. In Sentinel, classified articles, source attribu-
tion, revisions, publication review and expiry provide a way to maintain that knowledge.
Retained uploads and recoverable processing extend acquisition without making every
submitted document approved organisational guidance. Saved conversations likewise re-
main separate from published knowledge, so reuse includes an editorial decision rather
than automatic promotion of generated text.
    RQ2 connects this knowledge to the analyst’s question. We combine embeddings from
pretrained models with lexical retrieval and indicator lookup, allowing semantic similar-
ity and explicit technical terms to contribute to passage selection. The language model
receives authorised source material and instructions to distinguish reported observations
from possible explanations, relate actions to their purpose and preserve uncertainty. Arti-
cle and passage references connect the response to inspectable knowledge. Machine learn-
ing supports representation and explanation; it does not determine publication authority
or execute remediation. When generation is unavailable, eligible playbook excerpts or
direct article references provide an alternative route to the existing guidance. This design
uses pretrained models at inference time rather than training a new incident-response
model.
    For RQ3, the documented engineering checks establish selected processing, access


                                                                                         31
and interface behaviours under their test conditions. The live provider calls reported
in Chapter 4 distinguish accepted requests from an embedding configuration rejected
at the requested dimension. These observations concern technical operation; they do
not quantify retrieval relevance, answer correctness or analyst eﬀiciency. We distinguish
those outcomes from usability and interpret task time alongside the quality of the resulting
decision. A resolved citation, a completed model request and a correct incident-response
decision answer different evaluation questions.

5.2. Contributions and limits

The principal contribution is the connection between maintained knowledge and an in-
cident question. An analyst can describe suspicious activity, consult retrieved guidance,
inspect its supporting passage and return to the broader procedure. Knowledge custodi-
ans can revise that procedure without retraining the language model. By bringing these
activities together, we give knowledge management a direct role in response assistance,
while keeping responsibility for the underlying content visible. The contribution is an inte-
gration of established methods around incident-response work, rather than a new retrieval
algorithm.
    The design also makes the distinction between source access and advice review concrete.
The reading interface exposes both the article and the particular passage associated with
an answer. This supports checking a recommendation against its context, but an exact
reference does not prove that every generated statement follows from the passage. Nor
does a relevant source establish that its procedure applies to the affected environment.
The analyst still needs to assess prerequisites, operational consequences and uncertainty
before acting.
    Several choices constrain the context available for that assessment. Fixed passage
boundaries and a limited generation context can separate an instruction from a qualifi-
cation. Literal-question retrieval can miss a follow-up that needs conversational context.
Publication and indexing occur separately, so an approved revision may temporarily be
unavailable to semantic search. Current access rules can also make a previously cited
passage unavailable. We accept these last two restrictions to avoid reusing obsolete or
no longer authorised material. The graph assertion store records evidence-linked relation-
ships; the assistant does not traverse it to retrieve knowledge.
    Deployment introduces a separate set of boundaries. Local chat or embedding infer-
ence does not establish that storage and identity services are local, and an external model
service receives the material sent to it. Access within the KMS and permission to dis-
close knowledge to a provider are therefore distinct decisions. Operational records also
remain best-effort rather than a guarantee of complete event capture. These limits affect
how the system can be used and investigated; they are not measures of model quality or
substitutes for assessing decisions in their operational setting.



32
5.3. Further development

We see useful extensions in preserving procedural context during passage construction
and improving follow-up retrieval without bypassing evidence eligibility. Structure-aware
passages could keep prerequisites with the actions they qualify. A revised conversational
search strategy could use earlier observations while retaining a clear response when ac-
cessible evidence is insuﬀicient. Both extensions address specific ways in which relevant
knowledge can be lost between a source and an answer.
    A more direct route from a reviewed incident lesson to a proposed article revision could
also make experience easier to retain, while leaving publication with the responsible custo-
dians. The aim would be to preserve why a decision was made and when it applies, not to
treat a saved conversation as automatically learned organisational knowledge. Linking an
exception to the procedure it changes would help the next responder identify the relevant
qualification.
    Sentinel provides an implemented approach to the architectural part of the principal
research question: maintained knowledge supplies the content, machine learning supports
its retrieval and explanation, and analysts decide how to use it. The criterion for optimi-
sation remains the quality of that decision together with the effort required to reach it.
This keeps incident-response knowledge reuse, rather than the fluency of generated text,
at the centre of the contribution.

We use the following constructed report to connect that lifecycle to an incident question:

     I followed a link in a message and entered my work password. I do not know
     whether anything else changed.

The report establishes what the person says they did. It does not establish whether the
account was subsequently accessed, which service was involved or whether the responder
has administrative authority. We leave these details unspecified rather than inventing
them to complete the scenario.

A.2.1. Knowledge needed for the decision

In this example, a phishing playbook distinguishes receiving a message from submitting
credentials or opening an executable file. Account-recovery guidance describes the appli-
cable procedure, while a local responsibility statement identifies who can authorise and
perform account changes. An earlier incident lesson could explain a relevant exception,
but only if its circumstances still apply. The documents serve different purposes: a general
threat description cannot replace the local authority or recovery procedure.

The example does not assume that all of these sources exist in every deployment. If a
necessary local procedure is absent, the missing context remains a limitation on the advice
rather than an invitation to invent an organisational policy.

A.2.2. Interaction and decision points

   1. Establish the observations. The analyst records the credential submission as
      reported and keeps account compromise as a hypothesis. Focused clarification can
      concern the affected service, whether a file was opened and which steps have already
      been taken. Passwords and verification codes are not needed for this description.

   2. Locate applicable knowledge. The incident description supplies the retrieval
      question. Semantic matching can connect everyday wording with a playbook, while
      explicit terms can support lexical matching. Access and publication rules determine
      which passages are eligible, independently of how strongly they match the topic.

   3. Interpret the guidance. The explanation distinguishes evidence-preservation and
      investigation steps from changes that require operational authority. Any proposed
      action needs its source-supported purpose and conditions. A recommendation to
      involve the responsible account team remains advice; the KMS does not perform
      the account change.

   4. Inspect the basis for action. The analyst opens the cited passage and, where
      needed, the wider procedure. This allows examination of prerequisites, qualifications
      and responsibility that a short explanation might omit. The decision depends on
      that applicability check, not on the presence of a citation marker alone.

   5. Retain the lesson. A saved conversation can support handover. If the case reveals
      an incomplete procedure, a custodian can propose a revision through the publication

     workflow. Retaining the conversation does not automatically change the playbook
     or train the model.

A.2.3. Changes that alter the interaction

If the first retrieval finds no usable evidence, the assistant directs the analyst to refine
the question or browse available playbooks. This differs from a generation outage after
relevant material has been found. In the latter situation, an eligible playbook can sup-
ply bounded excerpts labelled as existing guidance, or the assistant can provide article
references for direct reading. The excerpts do not become a personalised response merely
because they appear in the conversation.

reference and present disclosure visible to the analyst. It does not silently replace the
cited passage with a different revision.

    The condition record also gives deployment flexibility, N3, a concrete meaning in
interpretation: two evaluations can use the same chat model while differing in where
embeddings, knowledge and identity are processed. Access context relates to N1, while
the association between output, passage and assessment supports the reviewability sought
in N2. These descriptions concern what a record means, not a claim that every possible
configuration was compared.

B.4. Interpreting an aggregate summary

Retrieval statistics depend on both the reference set and the evaluation unit. A document-
level relevance set does not imply passage-level recall, and a reference set for an unre-
stricted identity may include material unavailable to another identity. The cutoff also
matters when interpreting rank-based results. The relevant set, access condition and scor-
ing definition therefore belong with the result rather than being implicit assumptions.
    For response quality, a citation can resolve correctly while the associated claim over-
states its source. Completeness requires attention to omitted conditions as well as in-
correct statements. Separate error categories preserve these distinctions: a misleading
recommendation cannot be understood from a citation count alone. Failed requests and
missing outputs likewise retain their declared outcome categories instead of disappearing
from the comparison.
    Task-time summaries depend on the recorded start and end events, not just the model’s
response latency. A shorter attempt can end in an incomplete or incorrect decision. Us-
ability responses describe another aspect of the interaction and retain the wording and


                                                                                          41
scoring rules of the actual instrument. Repeated model requests, repeated tasks by one
participant and observations from different participants are different units; their counts
cannot be substituted for one another.
    We read these records together to relate finding knowledge, explaining it and using it
in a response decision. This preserves the evaluation dimensions of RQ3 without allowing
an operational check, a subjective rating or a single aggregate score to stand for the whole
incident-response task.
